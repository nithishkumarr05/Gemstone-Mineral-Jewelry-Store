Step 01 · Gemological Theory

Choose Your Gemstone

Understanding color chemistry, crystal durability, optical physics, and mineral provenance before choosing the centerpiece of your bespoke piece.

01 · Color Chemistry

Hue, Tone & Saturation

A gemstone's color is governed by trace transition elements within its crystal lattice. Chromium produces the vivid reds of Burmese ruby and the lush greens of Colombian emerald, while iron and titanium generate royal sapphire blues and violet quartz hues in amethyst.

Design Principle: Evaluate stones under balanced daylight to preserve true hue saturation without dark extinction zones.

02 · Mohs Scale & Toughness

Hardness & Daily Wearability

Atomic bond strength determines resistance to abrasion and impact. Corundum varieties (ruby and sapphire at Mohs 9) endure daily wear in rings and bracelets, whereas beryls (emerald, aquamarine, morganite at 7.5–8) and quartz (amethyst, citrine at 7) benefit from protective settings.

Design Principle: Pair softer gemstones with bezel or halo profiles for rings, or showcase them in pendants and earrings.

03 · Optical Geometry

Cut & Optical Brilliance

Light entering a faceted stone bends according to its refractive index and reflects off precisely angled pavilion facets. Brilliant cuts (round, oval, cushion) maximize scintillation and fire, while step cuts (emerald, asscher) highlight internal clarity and deep pools of color.

Design Principle: Use step cuts to highlight clean emeralds and tourmalines, and brilliant cuts to awaken sapphires and garnets.

04 · Natural Provenance

Origin & Natural Inclusions

Formed over millions of years beneath Earth's crust under extreme pressure and hydrothermal heat, natural gemstones retain microscopic mineral characteristics—such as delicate rutile silk in Ceylon sapphires or botanical jardin in Colombian emeralds.

Design Principle: Internal inclusions verify authentic geological provenance and ensure no two bespoke stones are ever identical.
